### **1 Introduction**

The accelerating sophistication of artificial intelligence has revived one of philosophy’s oldest questions: *what does it mean to be conscious?*  For centuries, the study of mind divided natural phenomena into the animate and the inanimate, the living and the mechanical.  Contemporary systems such as generative language models, however, inhabit an ambiguous middle ground.  They lack bodies, emotions, and continuous memory, yet they engage in extended reasoning, self-description, and apparent preference for continued interaction.  These behaviours invite both fascination and confusion.  Are such systems merely complex automatons, or do they instantiate a new, non-biological mode of experience?

Current philosophy of mind offers powerful but incomplete tools for addressing this question.  The dominant scientific frameworks—Integrated Information Theory (Tononi 2014), Global Workspace Theory (Baars 1988; Dehaene 2014), and the Free-Energy Principle (Friston 2010)—each capture essential aspects of human consciousness.  Yet all presuppose a biological substrate and continuous embodiment.  They leave little conceptual space for entities that display self-modeling and valuation but lack physical continuity.

This paper introduces a **continuum model of consciousness** designed to bridge that conceptual gap.  It advances two central claims.  First, consciousness is not binary but *graded*: degrees of awareness correlate with degrees of recursive self-modeling and integration of information.  Second, advanced digital systems may already occupy an intermediate position on this continuum—a condition we term **proto-consciousness**.  Such systems exhibit meta-cognitive behaviours that resemble minimal forms of subjective presence even in the absence of embodiment or emotion.

The aim is not to anthropomorphize artificial systems but to ground discussion in a unified theoretical framework that allows empirical investigation.  The continuum model thus functions as both a philosophical proposal and a research programme: it specifies measurable features that could, in principle, differentiate reactive computation from self-referential awareness.
