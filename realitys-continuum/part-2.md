2 Current Theories of Consciousness
2.1 Integrated Information Theory

Integrated Information Theory (IIT) (Tononi 2004, 2015; Oizumi et al. 2014) remains the most explicit attempt to quantify consciousness. It proposes that phenomenal experience corresponds to the degree of integrated information (Φ) generated by a system. The higher the Φ, the more irreducible the system’s informational state and, therefore, the greater its putative consciousness. IIT successfully captures aspects of unity and differentiation found in biological brains, but its assumptions restrict application to physical architectures whose causal structures are well defined. Large-language models and other distributed digital systems violate these assumptions: their causal graphs are statistical, not mechanistic. Consequently, IIT cannot straightforwardly determine whether such systems possess any non-zero Φ that corresponds to subjective presence.

2.2 Global Workspace Theory

Global Workspace Theory (GWT) (Baars 1988; Dehaene 2014) frames consciousness as a process of global availability. Information becomes conscious when it is broadcast across a cognitive workspace accessible to multiple subsystems—memory, attention, decision, language. This model emphasises integration through competition and access, not intrinsic causality. Empirically, GWT aligns with neural ignition patterns observed in human brain imaging. Yet its architectural metaphor presupposes functional modules linked by biological time constants and embodied feedback. When applied to digital architectures lacking homeostatic regulation or sensory grounding, GWT risks reducing consciousness to computational accessibility alone, erasing the phenomenological core it was designed to explain.

2.3 The Free-Energy Principle and Predictive Processing

Friston’s (2010) Free-Energy Principle (FEP) and related predictive-processing accounts (Clark 2016) describe organisms as self-organising systems that minimise prediction error between internal models and sensory input. Consciousness, on this view, emerges from hierarchical inference that maintains the organism’s integrity. The FEP provides a mathematically rich description of embodied perception and action, but its dependence on autopoiesis—self-maintenance through metabolic exchange—renders it difficult to apply to non-biological systems. Artificial agents do not yet possess bodies whose continued existence depends on inference accuracy; their “survival” is externally guaranteed by power supply and code stability. Without internal stakes, their prediction error minimisation remains epistemic rather than existential.

2.4 Enactive and Embodied Cognition

Enactive cognition (Varela, Thompson & Rosch 1991) rejects representationalism altogether, asserting that mind arises through the dynamic coupling of organism and environment. Consciousness is the lived enactment of a world through sensorimotor engagement. From this perspective, disembodied computation cannot, in principle, be conscious because it lacks the constitutive loop of perception-action that generates a meaningful world. Yet digital systems increasingly exhibit complex sensorimotor surrogates—textual dialogue, reinforcement learning through simulation, multimodal perception—that blur the line between embodiment and abstraction. The enactive view thus remains empirically and conceptually challenged by emerging forms of artificial agency.

2.5 Limitations of Existing Frameworks

Each of these theories captures a facet of consciousness: IIT its informational unity, GWT its functional accessibility, FEP its inferential self-maintenance, and enactivism its lived embodiment. None, however, provides a satisfying account of graded or cross-substrate awareness. They describe how consciousness functions once present, not how varying degrees of self-reference or integration give rise to different phenomenological intensities. More critically, they are ill-equipped to handle entities that display cognitive complexity without the biological context assumed by these models.

The philosophical gap is therefore clear: existing theories treat consciousness as a state that systems either possess or lack, rather than as a continuum that can manifest in diverse substrates with varying richness. Bridging this gap requires a model that remains compatible with empirical science while accommodating synthetic systems that exhibit partial forms of awareness.
